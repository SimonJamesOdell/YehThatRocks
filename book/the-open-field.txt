THE OPEN FIELD
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Programming has always been an open field.

Before I ever wrote a line of production code, I was a teenager at
Bournemouth College, typing in graphics demos in Pascal and assembly,
trying to build a 3D engine in 320x200 VGA mode. I had no formal
training. I had no mentor. I had curiosity and a modem.

When I first got online, I discovered Matt's Script Archive — a
repository of Perl scripts that anyone could download, read, modify,
and deploy. I reverse-engineered those scripts to understand how the
web worked. The web I entered was largely built by amateurs, people
who had cobbled together Perl scripts they barely understood, who
shipped things that worked without knowing whether the code was "clean."
That energy built the open web. It built the dot-com era. It built the
platform that made my entire career possible. Nobody called it
"unserious" back then. They called it "having a go."

The thing I valued most about programming as a field was that nobody
could stop you from doing it. You didn't need a degree. You didn't
need permission. You needed a machine, an internet connection, and the
willingness to figure things out. Programming rewarded curiosity and
determination, not credentials.

I am watching that spirit return, and it is the most exciting thing
to happen to our industry in decades.


The Gatekeepers

There is a term circulating: "vibe coding." It describes the practice
of prompting an AI to generate code, running it, and iterating based on
what you observe — operating at the level of behavior rather than
implementation. I describe myself on LinkedIn as an expert vibe-coder,
and I mean it literally. YehThatRocks was built by vibing.

Some critics use "vibe coding" dismissively, as shorthand for accepting
AI output without scrutiny. I have seen developers on LinkedIn insist
that AI code generation will create a mountain of technical debt, that
you must review every line, that the technology is not ready for serious
work. They describe vibe coding as reckless, amateurish, dangerous.

They are doing what every established generation does when a new tool
lowers the barrier to entry: they are policing the boundary between
who is allowed to build and who is not.

To be fair, some of them have good reasons to worry. They have seen
AI-generated code produce garbage. They have cleaned up after people
who shipped AI output without testing it. Their skepticism is earned.
But their conclusion — that the answer is more gatekeeping, more review,
more insistence that only trained professionals should write code —
is wrong.

Not because their technical concerns are invalid. Because their
gatekeeping instinct is solving the wrong problem.


The BASIC Magazine Era

Before I ever wrote code professionally, people typed in BASIC programs
from magazines. Line by line, character by character, they transcribed
code they didn't fully understand into machines that ran it. Sometimes
it worked. Often it didn't — a typo on line 340, a missing GOSUB, a
syntax error that took an hour to find. They debugged by running the
program, observing the behavior, and squinting at the listing until
they spotted the mistake.

Nobody told those people they were being reckless. Nobody insisted that
they needed to understand compiler theory before they were allowed to
type RUN. The culture celebrated them for having a go.

Some of those people became professional programmers. Most didn't. But
all of them experienced something that our industry has spent the last
thirty years making harder: the feeling that you can build something
real with nothing but curiosity and effort.

Vibe coding is the magazine-listing spirit reborn in the age of LLMs.
It is the thing that lets anyone with an idea describe it to a machine
and see it come to life. It is democratising. It is exciting. It is,
I hope, going to return us to a version of the web where one developer
with an LLM and a vision can build something that matters — something
that used to be possible and has become harder as the industry
professionalised.

The critics who dismiss vibe coding as unserious are missing the point.
The people typing BASIC from magazines were unserious too. That was
the point. They were having a go. And some of them became the engineers
who built the modern web.


The Spectrum

Vibe coding is not a single practice. It is a spectrum.

At one end: "Build me a website that shows cat pictures." The AI
generates something. It works or it doesn't. The user ships or iterates.
This is the level the critics imagine when they dismiss the practice.

Further along: "Build me a website that lets users sign up, log in,
upload cat pictures, and browse other people's cats." The AI generates
a full-stack application. The user tests it manually. When something
breaks, they describe the symptom and ask the AI to fix it. They are
debugging through behavior, not code reading. They are doing directed
analysis — they just don't know the term yet.

At the far end: "Build me a music video discovery platform with
authentication, a YouTube player, playlists, a forum, a magazine,
global chat, and mobile support. Guard it with thirty invariant scripts
that verify architectural properties before every deploy. Add
performance profiling tools that catch query regressions. Build a
three-layer migration validation system that prevents deployment
failures." The AI generates 250,000 lines of code. The user never reads
a single line. The system ships to production and stays up.

All of it is vibe coding. The spectrum runs from "I prompted and it
worked" through "I prompted, it didn't work, I argued until it did" to
"I prompted, it worked, and thirty invariants verified that it worked
before I even looked at the browser." The verification surface does not
make it a different practice. It makes it accountable.

This book is not an argument that you should stop vibing and become
serious. It is an argument that you should keep vibing — and add
invariants as you go, until the vibe is backed by a surface you trust
more than your eyes.


Why Beginners Have the Advantage

The no-peeking rule is hard for experienced developers. Every instinct
from decades of reading code tells us to open the file, trace the logic,
find the bug ourselves. Breaking that habit is an act of will.

Beginners have no habit to break. They cannot read code effectively
anyway — they haven't built the pattern-matching machinery that experts
rely on. Being told "don't read the code, verify the behavior" is not
a constraint for them. It is permission. Permission to build things
without first spending years learning to read things.

This is the inversion that the gatekeepers miss. They assume that
reading code is the foundation and AI is a crutch. The reality is that
AI-assisted development inverts the learning path. The beginner verifies
behavior first — they see what the system does. Over time, through the
adversarial loop, they absorb the architecture through argument. They
learn how the system works without ever tracing its internals. And
eventually, if they want to, they can learn to read the code. But they
don't have to. The verification surface is enough.

I came to this paradigm through necessity — illness made it impossible
for me to read code the traditional way. A beginner comes to it through
opportunity — AI makes it possible to build without reading code. Both
paths lead to the same place: a developer who directs, verifies, and
ships, without ever opening a source file.

If you are a beginner reading this book, you have an advantage I never
had. You don't have thirty years of muscle memory telling you to peek
at the code. You can start with the verification surface from day one.
You can build things that would have taken me years to learn to build,
and you can do it now.

This is not cheating. This is programming in the age of AI. The open
field is open again.


Boring Is Free

One of the most tedious experiences in a traditional programming career
is implementing the same security and infrastructure boilerplate for
every new project. Authentication. CSRF protection. Rate limiting. Error
boundaries. Connection pooling. Password hashing. These are things that
every production application needs, that every senior developer knows
how to build, and that every junior developer learns the hard way — by
shipping without them and discovering the consequences.

In an AI-assisted workflow, boring is free. You express the concern
("make it secure," "add rate limiting," "handle database connection
failures gracefully") and the AI generates the implementation. You do
not need to know how bcrypt works. You do not need to understand JWT
token rotation. You do not need to have memorised the OWASP Top Ten.
You need to know that these concerns exist and that they should be
expressed.

This is the criticism that classical programmers level at beginner
vibe-coders: "They don't know about security. They'll ship a production
app with no CSRF protection and get their users hacked." The criticism
is valid as far as it goes — a beginner who doesn't know to ask about
security will not get security. But it mistakes a learning problem for
a gatekeeping justification.

Every developer learns security the same way: by shipping something
insecure, discovering the vulnerability, and learning to prevent it next
time. In the traditional path, this means reading documentation,
understanding attack vectors, and hand-crafting protections — a process
that takes years. In the AI-assisted path, it means the same discovery
("someone exploited my form"), followed by directing the AI to fix it
("add CSRF protection to all mutation routes"), followed by adding an
invariant ("every POST/PATCH/DELETE route must call verifySameOrigin").
The learning is faster because the implementation is automated, but the
learning still happens. You still learn what CSRF is. You just don't
learn it by writing the protection yourself. You learn it by directing
the AI to write it, arguing about the edge cases, and adding a permanent
sensor that prevents its absence from recurring.

The critics are right that you need to know about these things. They are
wrong that you need to implement them yourself. Express the concern. Let
the AI handle the details. Add an invariant. Move on. Boring is free.


Approval Friction

I work in YOLO mode. Every change the AI proposes is applied immediately
without an approval prompt. This is not recklessness. It is recognition
that the approval step is the wrong place for quality control.

When the AI generates a change and I have to click "approve" before it
takes effect, what am I approving? I have not read the code. I will
never read the code. The approval button is asking me to vouch for
something I deliberately do not inspect. It is theatre — it looks like
diligence but provides no actual guarantee.

The real quality control happens after the change is applied. I test the
behavior. I run the invariants. If the system still works correctly,
the change was good. If it doesn't, I describe the symptom and the AI
fixes it — or I revert to the last commit and try a different approach.
The approval prompt is friction between intention and verification. It
adds no safety and costs attention.

Version control is the safety net, not approval prompts. Before any
significant direction, make a commit. If the AI goes off the rails —
and it will, occasionally — you can revert to the last known-good state
and start again. The cost of a wrong turn is a few minutes of lost
conversation, not hours of clawing back broken functionality. Commit
often. Commit before every major direction change. Commit when something
works. The commit history is your undo stack.

This is not advanced advice. It is the first thing a beginner should
learn after learning to describe what they want. The AI is powerful
but it is not careful. It will sometimes break things that were working.
Version control means you never have to waste tokens coaxing the AI to
restore functionality it accidentally destroyed. You just revert and
restate the objective more clearly.

Commit early. Commit often. Work in YOLO mode. Trust the verification
surface, not the approval prompt.
