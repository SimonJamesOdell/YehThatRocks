CHAPTER 5
The Adversarial Loop
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

The AI does not get things right the first time. This is not a weakness
of the approach — it is the approach.

Every feature, every bug fix, every architectural change follows the same
pattern: describe, argue, converge, verify. The adversarial loop is the
engine that produces correct software without human code reading. This
chapter describes how it works and why it requires skills that
experienced developers already possess.


The Shape of a Session

A directed AI analysis session has a rhythm:

1. COMMISSION. You describe what you want. Not how to implement it — what
   the system should do, what properties it should have, what behavior
   should change. The AI generates code. You do not read it. You test it.

2. OBSERVE. You interact with the running system. You click the buttons.
   You check the invariants. You look at the output. Something is wrong —
   a feature doesn't work, an invariant fails, a behavior is missing.

3. DESCRIBE. You tell the AI what you observed. You are specific: "When
   I click the Watch Now button on the magazine page, the player loads
   a video I've already hidden." Not "the magazine page is broken."

4. ARGUE. The AI proposes an explanation. You evaluate it. Does it
   account for everything you observed? Does it make sense given what
   you know about the system? If not, you push back. You explain why
   the explanation is insufficient. You demand a better one.

   ( there is no point ever getting angry with the AI, or calling it 
    names... just saying... )

5. CONVERGE. After one or more rounds of argument, the AI reaches an
   explanation that accounts for the evidence. You recognize it as
   correct because it explains everything you observed and nothing
   contradicts it. You direct the AI to fix it.

6. VERIFY. The AI implements the fix. You test it again. The symptom
   is gone. The invariants pass. You direct the AI to add a new
   invariant that prevents this class of failure from recurring.

7. REPEAT. The next feature, the next bug, the next session. The loop
    tightens as the surface grows. Fewer things go wrong. When they do,
    they are caught faster and fixed with less argument.


The Human Sensor

Before invariants catch anything, you catch it.

The adversarial loop starts with human observation, not automated
verification. You are the first sensor. You click the buttons. You
navigate the pages. You notice that the Watch Now link goes to the wrong
video, that the mobile layout shows the artist name twice, that the page
takes three seconds to load. No invariant would have caught these things
— because you hadn't commissioned it yet.

Manual QA is not a temporary phase that the verification surface
eventually replaces. It is the permanent front end of the sensor
network. The invariants catch regressions — things that used to work and
have stopped. Manual QA catches new failures — things that have never
worked correctly, or things that have degraded in ways no existing
sensor covers.

This is the bridge between simple vibe coding and directed AI analysis.
In vibe coding, the developer observes a problem and prompts the AI to
fix it. In directed AI analysis, the developer observes a problem,
prompts the AI to fix it, and then commissions an invariant so that
class of problem can never recur. The observation step is identical.
The difference is what happens after the fix.

For beginners, this means you do not need to build a verification
surface before you start building features. You start by vibing. You
describe what you want. You test it manually. When it breaks, you
describe the symptom and the AI fixes it. After a few cycles, you will
notice that the same kinds of things keep breaking. That is the moment
to add your first invariant. The verification surface grows organically
from the failures you actually observe, not from a theoretical list of
things that might go wrong.

For experienced developers, manual QA is the same skill you have always
used — testing your own work, noticing when behavior is off, forming
hypotheses about causes. The difference is that instead of opening the
code to investigate, you describe the symptom to the AI and argue. Your
QA skills are more valuable than ever. They are the front end of the
entire quality assurance pipeline.

Manual QA is not a weakness of the approach. It is the approach. The
verification surface amplifies your observations. It does not replace
them.


Why Argument Matters

The AI's first explanation is usually a surface-level description of
what happened, not a root-cause analysis. "The wrong video loaded because
the state was stale." This is not an explanation — it is a restatement
of the symptom in different words.

Your job is to reject insufficient explanations until the AI reaches
a genuine root cause. "The state was stale because the SSR path made a
playback decision that the client path was supposed to make, and the
two paths disagreed about which video was current."

Getting to this level requires pushing back at each layer of the AI's
reasoning. You must ask: why was the state stale? What set it? What
should have set it instead? How do we prevent this from happening in any
other route?

The AI will answer each question. Some answers will be wrong. You will
identify the wrong ones by their failure to account for the evidence
or by their inconsistency with what you know about the system's
architecture. You will push back. The AI will try again.

This is not magic. It is the Socratic method applied to a machine. You
ask questions until the machine reaches a conclusion you can verify.


The Skills You Need

The adversarial loop requires skills that experienced developers already
have:

Hypothesis evaluation. You must be able to look at a proposed explanation
and determine whether it accounts for the evidence. This is the same skill
you use when debugging your own code: "If the cache were stale, the
symptom would be consistent across reloads. But it's intermittent.
Therefore the cache is not the cause."

Architectural knowledge. You must understand your system's architecture
well enough to recognize when the AI's explanation violates it. "The
magazine page shouldn't be making playback decisions at all. Why is it?"
This knowledge comes from having specified the architecture, not from
having read the code.

Persistence. The AI will propose wrong explanations. It will repeat
itself. It will miss obvious connections. You must keep pushing until
it converges. This is the hardest skill to develop and the most
important.

Bullshit detection. Experience has calibrated your sense of when
something doesn't add up. The AI says "it might be a timing issue."
You know to ask "timing of what relative to what?" The AI's answer
will either be specific enough to act on or vague enough to reject.
Your bullshit detector is the filter.


A Note on Humility

The adversarial loop requires a posture that does not come naturally
to most experienced developers: you must trust the AI's reading of the
code more than you trust your own, if you were to read it.

This is uncomfortable. You have spent decades believing that your ability
to read and understand code is your most valuable skill. It is. But the
AI can do it faster and more comprehensively. Your most valuable skill
in this paradigm is not reading code — it is evaluating explanations,
forming hypotheses, and directing analysis.

The AI is the code reader. You are the director. The director does not
operate the camera, but the film is theirs. The director does not need
to know which lens was used, but they need to know whether the shot
conveys the right emotion.

You do not read the code. You read the AI's reasoning about the code.
And your decades of experience make you a very good reader of reasoning.
