CHAPTER 3
Directed AI Analysis
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

You do not read the code. But you do read the AI's reasoning about the
code. This is a different kind of reading, and it is the core skill of
the paradigm.

When something goes wrong — a feature doesn't work, an invariant fails,
a production incident occurs — the traditional developer opens the
relevant source files and traces the logic. They form hypotheses about
what might be wrong, test those hypotheses by reading more code, and
eventually identify the root cause.

In the directed AI analysis workflow, you do something different. You
describe the symptom to the AI. You observe the AI's explanation. You
argue with it when the explanation doesn't account for the evidence.
You push back, you demand alternative hypotheses, you insist on root
causes rather than surface fixes. You do not read the code — but you
interrogate the AI's understanding of the code until it converges on
the truth.

This is genuine intellectual labor. It requires all the skills of
traditional debugging — hypothesis formation, evidence evaluation,
logical reasoning — but applied to a conversation rather than a codebase.


The Conversation as Debugger

A traditional debugging session looks like this:

1. Observe symptom.
2. Form hypothesis about cause.
3. Read relevant code to test hypothesis.
4. If hypothesis is wrong, return to step 2.
5. If hypothesis is right, implement fix.
6. Verify fix resolves symptom.

A directed AI analysis session looks like this:

1. Observe symptom.
2. Describe symptom to AI.
3. AI proposes an explanation.
4. Evaluate explanation against observed behavior.
5. If explanation is wrong, explain why and demand a better one.
6. If explanation is right, direct AI to implement fix.
7. Direct AI to add invariant preventing recurrence.
8. Verify fix resolves symptom and invariant catches it.

The difference is who reads the code. In traditional debugging, you read
it. In directed analysis, the AI reads it and you evaluate the AI's
conclusions.

This is faster for two reasons. First, the AI reads faster than you do.
Second, the AI's reading is comprehensive — it doesn't skip sections,
it doesn't get tired, it doesn't assume things are fine because they
look familiar. When the AI reports that a certain function is the root
cause, it has actually read that function and traced its callers. When
you report the same thing after traditional debugging, you have probably
read a fraction of what the AI read.


The Adversarial Stance

The most important skill in directed AI analysis is the willingness to
argue. The AI's first explanation is often wrong. Its second explanation
is sometimes wrong. Its third explanation is usually close.

You must treat every AI explanation as a hypothesis to be tested, not a
conclusion to be accepted. You must demand evidence. You must identify
inconsistencies and push back. You must insist that the explanation
accounts for all observed behavior, not just the most obvious symptom.

This adversarial stance is not hostility. It is the same critical
thinking you apply to your own debugging hypotheses. The difference is
that you are applying it to someone else's reasoning — someone who reads
faster than you but reasons less carefully.

Experienced developers have an advantage here. Two decades of debugging
have calibrated your bullshit detector. You know what a plausible bug
sounds like and what a hand-waving explanation sounds like. When the AI
says "it might be a race condition," you know to ask "between what and
what, and under what timing?" When the AI says "the cache is stale," you
know to ask "what cache, what key, and what TTL?"

The AI generates explanations. You filter them. Together, you converge
on the truth faster than either could alone.


Vibe Coding and the Verification Surface

There is a term circulating in the industry: "vibe coding." It describes
the practice of prompting an AI to generate code, running it, and
iterating based on what you observe — operating at the level of behavior
rather than implementation. I describe myself on LinkedIn as an expert
vibe-coder, and I mean it literally. YehThatRocks was built by vibing.

Some critics use "vibe coding" dismissively, as shorthand for accepting
AI output without scrutiny. That version of the term deserves the
criticism it gets — but it is not what most people who call themselves
vibe-coders are actually doing. Most of them are doing what I am doing:
describing what they want, watching what happens, and course-correcting
when it goes wrong. They are debugging through behavior, not through
code reading. They are just doing it without the verification surface.

And that is not a moral failing. It is a starting point.

Before I ever got going with programming, people used to type in BASIC
programs they found in magazines. Programming has always been a wide open
field, one where anyone with the right aptitude, curiosity and determination
can go from building BASIC programs to writing professional quality 
software. No gate-keepers, no moat, no annointed masters deciding which
accolytes deserve the right to practice the craft.

When I got my first modem and started learning how the web works, the web 
I entered was largely built by amateurs, people who had reverse-engineered 
Perl scripts they barely understood, who shipped things that worked without
knowing whether the code was "clean." That energy built the open web. It 
built the dot-com era. It built the platform that made my entire career 
possible. Nobody called it "unserious" back then. They called it "having a go."

Vibe coding is the modern equivalent. It is the thing that lets anyone
with an idea describe it to a machine and see it come to life. It is
the magazine-listing spirit reborn in the age of LLMs. It is
democratising. It is exciting. It is, I hope, going to return us to
a version of the web where one developer with an LLM and a vision can
build something that matters — something that used to be possible and
has become harder and harder as the industry professionalised.

I am a vibe-coder. This is not an admission of unprofessionalism.

I am a vibe-coder who added a verification surface.

An expert vibe-coder.

Directed AI analysis is what happens when you take the vibe-coding
impulse — describe what you want, watch what happens, iterate — and
give it a sensor network. You're still vibing. You're still not reading
the code. You're still operating at the level of behavior and
conversation. But now, when the system does something wrong, you don't
just notice it manually. An invariant catches it. And when you fix it,
you add an invariant so that class of failure can never recur. The vibe
gets more reliable over time, not less.

This is not a different thing. It is the same thing, matured. The
spectrum runs from "I prompted and it worked" through "I prompted, it
didn't work, I argued until it did" to "I prompted, it worked, and
thirty invariants verified that it worked before I even looked at the
browser." All of it is vibe coding. The verification surface just makes
it accountable.

If you are a vibe-coder today — if you are building things with AI, not
reading the code, iterating on behavior — you are already doing directed
AI analysis. You are just doing it with a smaller sensor network. This
book is not an argument that you should stop vibing and become serious.
It is an argument that you should keep vibing, and add invariants as you
go, until the vibe is backed by a surface you trust more than your eyes.


A Real Example

During the development of YehThatRocks, the magazine page occasionally
showed a "Watch Now" button that linked to the wrong video. The symptom
was intermittent and hard to reproduce.

In a traditional workflow, I would have opened the magazine page
component, traced the video resolution logic, and eventually discovered
that the server-side rendering path was making a playback decision that
the client-side path was supposed to make.

In the directed analysis workflow, I described the symptom to the AI:
"On the magazine page, the Watch Now button sometimes links to the wrong
video. It doesn't happen every time. It seems related to page load."

The AI's first explanation was about client-side state management.
I pushed back: "If it were client-side state, it would be consistent
within a session. This varies between page loads."

The AI's second explanation identified the SSR playback decision path
as the likely cause. I asked: "Why would SSR make a playback decision
at all? The magazine page shouldn't need one."

The AI traced the dependency chain and found that a shared component
was triggering a playback decision during server rendering. The fix
was to skip the playback decision during SSR for that specific route.

Then I directed the AI to add a source invariant: "Playback decisions
must not be made during server-side rendering for read-only routes."
The invariant now catches this class of failure in any route, not just
the magazine page.

Total time: about twenty minutes. Lines of code read: zero.
